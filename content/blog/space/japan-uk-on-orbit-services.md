Japan and the UK have agreed to begin discussions on international on-orbit services. Officials from both countries met online on 11 November for their first space consultation, and Japan’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs published the readout the next day.

<!--more-->

My interest in these talks is a practical one. A space company looking to sell to customers or work with partners abroad still operates under national controls on its operations and its technology. Even after getting through the initial geopolitical barriers, regulatory requirements and import and export controls can pile up, adding overhead to cross-border business. Depending on the mission, a company may have to meet requirements in more than one country.

Government discussions could help clarify how those requirements fit together. The question is whether these talks produce arrangements companies can use.

## Why on-orbit services are interesting

On-orbit services bring commercial ambition into close contact with national security concerns. A company’s work may involve approaching or interacting with another spacecraft, making confidence in how it operates especially important.

Japan’s policy addresses that tension directly. Its March 2024 policy on orbital-use rule-making identifies the safety risks of spacecraft approaching each other and the civilian and security uses of servicing technology. It also connects the international adoption of Japan’s servicing guidelines with helping its space industry expand abroad. Establishing accepted ways of operating is part of creating the conditions for companies to do business internationally. [Japan’s policy on orbital-use rule-making](https://www8.cao.go.jp/space/taskforce/debris/stm/dai3/sankou3.pdf)

Japan was already considering how that coordination might work before this meeting. A May 2026 Cabinet Office briefing describes a study group examining coordination items among states and companies for future international on-orbit servicing. That work has a multilateral dimension; the published material does not establish a connection to the Japan–UK consultation. [Cabinet Office briefing](https://www8.cao.go.jp/space/comittee/dai123/sankou3r.pdf)

The UK has industrial and strategic reasons to care too. Its September space strategy prioritises in-orbit servicing, assembly and manufacturing, linking the broader field to industrial competitiveness, satellite life extension, resilience and defence. These are policy ambitions, with commercial benefits still to be demonstrated through projects and operations. [UK Space Strategy](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/uk-space-strategy/uk-space-strategy)

That makes these talks interesting to follow. Both countries are developing activities where international business depends partly on confidence in how companies operate around other objects in space. Cooperation could help those businesses pursue opportunities abroad while governments retain control over sensitive activities and technology.

## What that means for a company

Japan’s existing servicing guidance gives a concrete example of the requirements a company may encounter.

The Cabinet Office guideline addresses consent from the rights holder of the object being serviced. It also says a Japanese servicing licence does not replace the procedures required by the country that registered or licensed the satellite receiving the service. The requirements depend on the service and the rules that apply, but a company’s own authorisation may be only one part of preparing a mission. [Japan’s on-orbit servicing guideline](https://www8.cao.go.jp/space/application/space_activity/documents/guideline_oosgl.pdf)

The UK also maintains controls on its operators and on what they share. Its Outer Space Act regulates relevant spaceflight activities conducted overseas by UK entities. The Civil Aviation Authority’s orbital operator assessment includes national-security questions alongside technical and financial ones. Export controls can also affect technical collaboration, covering technology, information and technical assistance depending on the item and circumstances. [CAA orbital operator guidance](https://www.caa.co.uk/space/licences-and-permissions/orbital-operator/), [UK export-control guidance](https://www.gov.uk/find-licences/export-control-licence)

For a company pursuing an international project, these requirements sit alongside the commercial work of finding a customer and agreeing on a service. Understanding how the applicable procedures fit together matters when deciding whether a project can proceed.

## What the consultation could add

The readout records an agreement to begin “an exchange of views among relevant authorities regarding international on-orbit services.” It places that exchange alongside a shared view on developing international rules and norms among allies and like-minded countries. [MOFA’s consultation readout](https://www.mofa.go.jp/fp/msp/pagewe_000001_00001.html)

A bilateral exchange gives the two governments a forum to explore practical questions about cooperation. The published readout leaves its remit open: it does not define the services covered, identify the authorities assigned to the exchange or announce a timetable or mission. Licensing, export controls and liability are relevant operating conditions discussed here; they have not been announced as the exchange’s specific topics.

Space and security institutions participated on both sides. Japan’s delegation included ministries responsible for foreign affairs, industry and defence, alongside the Cabinet Office and JAXA. The UK’s included the UK Space Agency, its National Security Secretariat and the Ministry of Defence. Their participation shows the range of interests involved, although it does not establish who will lead the services exchange.

Commercial development is also part of the consultation’s stated context. The UK government’s October joint statement listed it among the priorities, alongside space security and sustainability. [UK–Japan joint statement](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/uk-japan-science-and-technology-joint-committee-joint-statement)

There is a procedural example elsewhere in the relationship. The same statement reports that a mutual-recognition scheme simplifying application procedures between Japan’s JC-STAR product-security labelling and the UK’s PSTI framework began in January 2026. It concerns product security, so it offers no ready-made model for a space mission. It does show the kind of specific administrative arrangement that can emerge from bilateral cooperation.

## Opening commercial opportunities

The published readout does not explicitly explain why the two governments chose to work on this topic in particular. I see its inclusion, alongside their existing policy priorities, as a sign of the growing importance they attach to this area. Their policies give us reasons to pay attention: industrial ambitions, sensitive technology and the need for accepted behaviour around other spacecraft all meet in this field.

For companies, the useful next detail is what the two governments intend to work on and how businesses might participate. That would help founders judge whether the discussions could address problems affecting their missions.

Government discussions could help companies work across borders within national rules. I’m interested to see whether Japan and the UK turn this exchange into arrangements that make cooperation easier and open up commercial opportunities for companies on both sides.
