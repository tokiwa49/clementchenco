All 27 EU member states have approved a collective framework for responding to threats in orbit, [according to Payload](https://payloadspace.com/europes-space-defenses-fall-just-shy-of-weaponization/), and the notable part is what the framework is not: a route to destructive counterspace weapons. Europe is choosing to build defense capability that stops short of blowing things up in orbit, and that choice quietly shifts the hard problem from policy to engineering.

<!--more-->

## What Europe is actually choosing

The policy logic is easy to follow. ESA Director General Josef Aschbacher has warned that damaging adversary spacecraft could create debris and make orbit less usable, which is a pointed argument coming from the head of an agency whose satellites would sit in that debris. A kinetic response against a satellite in a crowded regime is a response against the shared environment too.

So the interesting question is what Europe builds instead. Payload points to programmes such as BODYGUARD, which is exploring autonomous space situational awareness and non-destructive responses, including robotic or laser-based inspection and intervention. That list is worth reading slowly, because none of it is a single product. Sensing, autonomy, proximity operations and decision support are separate capabilities, and a credible non-destructive response needs all of them working together on a spacecraft that has to behave correctly with no operator in the loop for some of the time.

Credit where it's due: a framework that 27 governments signed up to is not a small thing, and choosing restraint over escalation is a defensible and arguably smart position. Nothing here should be read as skepticism about the direction.

## The gap between a framework and a capability

What the brief does not tell us is how much money, on what timeline, is attached to any of this. BODYGUARD is described as exploring these options, not fielding them, and the framework is about how to respond to threats, not a list of funded spacecraft. That's a normal place to be at this stage, but it means the demand signal is real while the procurement signal is still unclear.

The commercial side is moving in a similar direction. California startup Fortastra [raised a $30 million Series A](https://payloadspace.com/fortastra-raises-30m-series-a-to-fund-its-next-security-spacecraft/) for its next security spacecraft, has flown two demonstration missions since its 2025 founding (one on SpaceX's Transporter-18), and is positioning non-destructive options alongside more escalatory systems for dealing with adversarial satellites. Its next spacecraft could launch as early as Q1 2028. That timeline is a useful reference point: even a funded company with flight heritage is looking at roughly two years to its next vehicle. Fortastra is a US company and a separate market from European sovereign procurement, so it shouldn't be read as a forecast for BODYGUARD, only as an indication of how long this class of hardware takes to move from demo to operational use.

## Why modular proof matters more than a big program

Non-destructive defense is a stack of behaviors that each need evidence. A laser-based inspection payload, an autonomy stack that decides when to approach another object, and a situational awareness layer that decides what counts as a threat can each be demonstrated separately, and probably should be, because a failure in one is easier to diagnose when it flew alone.

That argues for staged, smaller in-orbit demonstrations whose results are documented well enough that a sovereign mission can later assemble them from components that already have flight history. It is a less glamorous path than a flagship defense satellite, and it fits the restraint Europe is signaling: capabilities you can test and verify are easier to trust than capabilities you only have to believe in.

The open question, and the one worth watching, is whether the EU framework turns into actual procurements for situational awareness, inspection and autonomy, or stays a statement of intent. If it does turn into orders, the winners will probably be whoever has flown the relevant piece first and can show the data.
