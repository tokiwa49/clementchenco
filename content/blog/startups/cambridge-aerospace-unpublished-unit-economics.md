Cambridge Aerospace, a UK defense-tech startup founded in 2024, raised $300 million on August 10 at a $3.4 billion valuation, a 2.6x markup on the $1.3 billion mark it set with a $200 million Series B just four months earlier, and the entire thesis behind that number rests on a unit-economics claim the company has never actually published, a gap [Silicon Canals flagged directly](https://siliconcanals.com/s-cambridge-aerospace-was-just-valued-at-3-4-billion-less-than-two-years-after-it-was-founded-and-the-two-numbers-that-would-let-anyone-test-that-valuation-the-price-of-one-skyhammer-and-the-size-of/) when the round closed.

<!--more-->

## The pitch is an economics claim, so the economics should be public

Start with what Cambridge Aerospace builds. Skyhammer is a 30-kilometer-range, 700-kilometer-per-hour turbojet-powered interceptor drone, pitched as a low-cost answer to the Shahed-style attack drones that Russia and other operators have been flying against Ukraine and, increasingly, against NATO's eastern flank. Those attack drones reportedly cost around $20,000 apiece. The legacy interceptors that air-defense batteries have traditionally used to shoot them down run closer to $1 million each. That asymmetry, cheap attacker against expensive defender, is the actual crisis NATO planners have been describing for the past two years: a saturation attack of a few dozen Shaheds can bankrupt a defense budget faster than it threatens the target, because every intercept costs fifty times more than the thing it's shooting down.

Skyhammer's entire reason to exist is closing that gap. The round led by DFJ Growth, with Lux Capital, Accel, Lakestar, Never Lift, Ora Global and Elad Gil & Co. participating, brought Cambridge Aerospace's total funding to more than $636 million. The UK Ministry of Defence has signed a contract for hundreds of units, and the system is already deployed with UK forces and Gulf partners, which is real, checkable progress for a company not yet two years old. None of that is in dispute.

What is in dispute, or would be if anyone outside the company could actually check it, is the number that determines whether Skyhammer solves the cost-asymmetry problem it was built to solve: how much does one Skyhammer actually cost, and how much is the UK paying for hundreds of them? Neither figure has ever been published. Co-founder Chris Sylvan has disclosed exactly one public performance number, a self-reported 70% effectiveness rate from company testing, cited at Eurosatory 2026, the defense trade show where the figure first surfaced. A self-reported success rate from a company's own testing, with no third-party replication, is a starting point for diligence, not a substitute for it.

## A billion-dollar valuation and a rate with no denominator

Here's why that specific omission matters more for Cambridge Aerospace than it would for most hardware startups. A 70% effectiveness rate is meaningless on its own. Seventy percent of what, tested how, against what kind of target, under what jamming or countermeasure conditions? And even taking the number at face value, effectiveness alone doesn't tell you whether Skyhammer beats the economics of a $1 million legacy interceptor. If a single Skyhammer costs $400,000, the cost-per-successful-intercept math (accounting for the 30% that miss) barely improves on the status quo. If it costs $40,000, the pitch holds up easily. Those are wildly different businesses, and wildly different reasons to price a company at $3.4 billion, and the only way to tell them apart is the one number Cambridge Aerospace has kept private.

That's the specific, narrow gap Silicon Canals called out in its own coverage of the round: the price of one Skyhammer and the size of the UK contract are the two figures that would let anyone outside the company independently test the $3.4 billion valuation, and neither has been made public. It's a sharper diligence question than "is this company overvalued," because it doesn't require guessing at a fair multiple. It just asks for the inputs to a calculation the company itself is implicitly making every time it pitches Skyhammer as the affordable answer to Shahed-style drones.

## Give the company its due, then ask the harder question

None of this means Cambridge Aerospace is overstating what it has built. A signed UK Ministry of Defence contract, live deployments with UK forces and Gulf partners, and a 2.6x valuation markup from a syndicate that includes Lux Capital and Accel are not nothing, and they're not the kind of thing sophisticated defense-tech investors write checks for on vibes alone. It's reasonable to assume DFJ Growth and its co-investors saw internal numbers during diligence that the public hasn't seen. The problem isn't that Cambridge Aerospace is necessarily wrong about its own economics. It's that the market, and the NATO customers this pitch is ultimately aimed at, are being asked to accept a $3.4 billion price on a single unverified number, at a moment when the underlying threat, mass drone saturation attacks, is real and urgent enough that it's tempting to wave through any company that looks like it's solving it.

That temptation is exactly where defense-tech valuations can run ahead of what's actually been proven. A live war creates real urgency, and real urgency is precisely the condition under which investors, and governments, are most likely to skip the step of asking a vendor to show its work. Cambridge Aerospace isn't the first defense company to raise a mega-round on a claim that hasn't been independently audited, and this isn't a special case of a company hiding something damning. It's a general pattern worth naming: [nine-figure defense and hard-tech rounds have started looking more like infrastructure finance](/blog/deeptech/capital-intensive-deeptech-is-starting-to-look-like-infrastructure-finance/) than traditional venture bets, backed by contracts and backlog rather than a roadmap slide, and the ones that hold up best are the ones willing to let outsiders check the specific number the whole thesis depends on.

## What would actually settle this

The fix here isn't complicated, and it's the same fix that would resolve most of the skepticism aimed at Cambridge Aerospace's round: publish a unit price, even a range, and publish what the UK contract is really worth. Plenty of defense contractors treat those numbers as commercially sensitive, and there are legitimate reasons a company selling into an active conflict zone might not want an adversary calculating exactly how many Skyhammers a given defense budget buys. But there's a difference between withholding operational specifics and withholding the one figure that lets an outside investor, journalist, or competing NATO government sanity-check a $3.4 billion price tag. Cambridge Aerospace has chosen the second kind of silence, and until that changes, the honest read on this round is that it's pricing a real and urgent problem on a number nobody outside the company has actually seen.

For founders raising into a category where the entire pitch is a cost-per-unit claim, the lesson here isn't to avoid ambition. It's that [how a funding round gets diligenced](/blog/deeptech/how-to-read-a-deeptech-funding-round/) increasingly depends on which of your numbers are checkable and which ones you're asking investors to take on faith, and the founders who publish the checkable number before anyone has to ask for it are the ones whose next round won't carry this same asterisk.

It's a question worth asking of more than one company at a time. [General Intuition's reported $6 billion valuation](/blog/startups/general-intuition-valuation-outruns-evidence/) is pricing a robotics thesis that's so far only been tested at its easiest difficulty setting, and [The Exploration Company's $450 million round](/blog/space/exploration-company-series-c-nyx-return-proof/) is pricing a narrow, targeted fix rather than a fully proven capsule. None of these are bad bets. All three are asking the market to trust a specific number the company itself hasn't fully opened up for outside inspection. Cambridge Aerospace's version of that number is just the one nobody outside the company has seen at all.

## What to watch next

Two things would change this calculus quickly. The first is whether Cambridge Aerospace ever publishes an actual Skyhammer unit price or discloses the UK contract's value, the two numbers Silicon Canals named as necessary to independently test the valuation. The second is whether the UK Ministry of Defence, or any of the Gulf partners already using the system, releases its own procurement figures, which would settle the question regardless of what the company chooses to disclose. Until one of those happens, $3.4 billion is a number built on a rate with no denominator.
