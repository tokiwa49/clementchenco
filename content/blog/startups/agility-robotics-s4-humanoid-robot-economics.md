Agility Robotics booked $1.8 million in revenue in 2025. It lost $140 million doing it. And the SPAC, a special purpose acquisition company, a shell corporation that raises cash on public markets first and finds an operating business to merge with second, that just disclosed both numbers still prices Agility at $2.5 billion, [a multiple north of 1,400 times trailing sales](https://www.therobotreport.com/agility-robotics-reports-18m-revenue-ahead-of-humanoid-spac/).

<!--more-->

That figure comes from the S-4, the SEC registration statement a company files to disclose financials and merger terms ahead of a stock-based business combination, that Agility submitted ahead of its merger with blank-check company Churchill Capital Corp XI, reported September 10. It's the first time actual revenue, burn and customer-concentration numbers, real financials rather than a pitch deck or a demo reel, have become public for any humanoid robotics company. Every other player in the category, Figure, Apptronik, Unitree and the rest, gets to keep its unit economics private for as long as it stays private. Agility, by virtue of choosing the SPAC route to get to public markets, just lost that privilege for everyone watching the category, whether it wanted the comparison drawn or not.

The SPAC route itself is worth pausing on, because it's not the path a company takes when it wants maximum scrutiny of a thin income statement. A traditional IPO runs through months of roadshow diligence with underwriters who have their own reputational skin in pricing the deal correctly. A SPAC merger lets a private company negotiate its valuation directly with a blank-check sponsor, then go public on that agreed number with comparatively lighter external price discovery. That's not a criticism of Agility specifically. It's the mechanical reason a $1.8 million revenue company can end up priced at $2.5 billion in the first place, and it's exactly why the market's first instinct on reading an S-4 like this one is to check whether the backlog story holds up once the number is finally sitting in public view.

## The numbers the filing actually discloses

The headline pairing is stark enough on its own: $1.8 million in 2025 net sales against a $140 million operating loss. Operating expenses climbed to $111 million from $71 million in 2024, and the company burned roughly $100 million in cash over the year, according to [the filing details reported by The Robot Report](https://www.therobotreport.com/agility-robotics-reports-18m-revenue-ahead-of-humanoid-spac/). The transaction is structured to bring in $620 million in gross proceeds: $420 million from Churchill's trust account and a $200 million PIPE, a private investment in public equity, where outside investors buy newly issued shares alongside the merger, led by Foxconn.

None of that is disqualifying on its own. Hardware companies burn cash for years before they scale, and $2.5 billion isn't an absurd number for a company that's actually shipping into production environments. Agility's own defense here is concrete: more than $300 million in multi-year orders for its Digit v5 humanoid from a single customer, and over 65,000 operational hours logged across deployments at Schaeffler, GXO, Toyota Motor Manufacturing Canada and Mercado Libre. Those aren't demo-day claims. Those are named enterprise customers running a humanoid robot in actual warehouse and manufacturing workflows, which is more than most of the category can currently point to.

## Where the valuation gets ahead of the evidence

But none of that operational proof shows up in the revenue line yet, and that's the problem the filing can't paper over. $300 million in contracted orders is backlog, not recognized revenue, and the filing itself puts 2025 net sales at $1.8 million. That gap, between a nine-figure order book and a seven-figure top line, is the entire question a public listing now forces into the open. Either the backlog converts on a normal enterprise-hardware timeline and the multiple compresses as revenue catches up, or it doesn't convert as fast as the deck implies and the market re-rates the stock hard on the first earnings call that comes up short.

A 1,400x revenue multiple isn't a valuation built on what Agility has sold. It's a valuation built on what Agility says it's about to sell, discounted back to today, and that distinction is worth naming plainly rather than letting the multiple speak for itself. That's a legitimate way to price a pre-scale hardware company with real committed orders in hand. It is not the same thing as a valuation the current income statement can defend on its own, and conflating the two is exactly the mistake an S-4 filing full of forward-looking backlog numbers is designed to invite.

The timing sharpens the point. The disclosure landed the same week the Bank for International Settlements [went public with a comparison](https://www.therobotreport.com/agility-robotics-reports-18m-revenue-ahead-of-humanoid-spac/) between current AI infrastructure capital spending and the dot-com bubble. That's not a comment on Agility specifically, and it shouldn't be read as a prediction that this deal ends the way overhyped 1999-era IPOs did. It's a comment on the standard the market is currently applying to any company asking to be priced on a growth story rather than a trailing financial statement, and Agility's S-4 landed squarely inside that scrutiny window whether the timing was intentional or not.

## What this does to everyone else in humanoid robotics

The more interesting second-order effect isn't about Agility at all. It's that every other private humanoid robotics company now has a public data point its investors, its competitors and its customers can benchmark against, whether it asked for that or not. Before this filing, "unit economics in humanoid robotics" was a private conversation between founders and their cap tables. After it, any VC doing diligence on a competing humanoid robotics deal has a real anchor: here's what $2.5 billion of enterprise-validated backlog and 65,000 hours of field deployment looks like translated into an actual income statement. If a competitor's numbers would look thinner than that next to the hype, that comparison is now available to anyone who wants to run it, on demand, for free.

That cuts in a specific direction for founders in the category. Going public, whether via SPAC or a traditional IPO, makes your economics permanently legible in a way that private fundraising never does. A private company can control the narrative around its numbers indefinitely, choosing what to disclose and to whom. A public filing hands that control to anyone with a Bloomberg terminal or a search engine. For a founder sitting on real backlog but thin trailing revenue, the decision to file isn't just about raising $620 million. It's a bet that the story survives being read literally by people with no stake in believing it.

## The credit due, and the question still open

Agility deserves credit here. Shipping 65,000 hours of humanoid robot operation across four named industrial customers is a hard, verifiable accomplishment that essentially no other company in the category can currently match with specifics attached. Digit v5 is deployed, not promised. The $300 million order book is disclosed with enough detail to be checked against reality over the next few quarters, which is more transparency than most late-stage private hardware companies ever volunteer. This is a company that has actually built and shipped a working product into paying customers' operations, which remains the hardest part of humanoid robotics and the part most competitors haven't cleared yet.

What isn't yet proven is the conversion rate: how much of that $300 million in orders becomes recognized revenue, on what timeline, and at what margin once Agility is scaling manufacturing rather than hand-building units for pilot deployments. The S-4 doesn't answer that question. It just makes it the first question anyone tracking the stock will be able to check against a real number, every quarter, in public, starting the moment the shareholder vote clears and the ticker goes live.
