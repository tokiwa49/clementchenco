Reverion plans to build a German factory with 250 megawatts of annual capacity, ten times what it produces today, and on September 29 it closed a [$175 million Series B](https://reverion.com/en/reverion-raises-175m-series-b-to-scale-production-of-its-dispatchable-carbon-negative-power-plants-tenfold/) to pay for it. The Munich-area company makes container-sized solid-oxide fuel cell plants, which convert gas or hydrogen into electricity electrochemically instead of burning it, and its new lead customer category is data centers, which increasingly can't wait for a grid connection.

<!--more-->

## What the round actually buys

The round was led by Kembara, with Allianz, KfW Capital (the German development bank's venture arm, investing through a vehicle managed by UVC), aurum Impact and Carbon Equity joining as new investors. Reverion was founded in 2022 and is based in Eresing, near Munich. Its plants run on biogas, natural gas or hydrogen and capture the CO₂ in pure form, which is how the company gets to calling its power "dispatchable" (available on demand, unlike solar or wind) and carbon-negative when fed biogas.

The proceeds fund the factory and, according to the company, up to 800 jobs. The euro figure varies by source, with [EU-Startups reporting €154.2 million](https://www.eu-startups.com/2026/09/germanys-reverion-raises-e154-2-million-to-scale-production-of-power-plants-that-generate-electricity-from-gas) and [Dealroom rounding to €150 million](https://dealroom.co/news/157462-reverion-lands-150m-to-build-a-fuel-cell-megafactory-for-the-ai-power-cr/), but that's currency conversion on the same $175 million, not a dispute about the round.

My claim is that this round is priced on Reverion's ability to build a factory, and that the two numbers carrying the story, a 74.2% electrical efficiency and a project pipeline above $2 billion, are still the company's own statements.

## Why data centers change the product

Fuel cells are not new, and for most of their history they were a niche technology with a patient customer base. What changed is the buyer. Grid interconnection queues, the years-long line of projects waiting for a utility to study and approve a connection, are pushing data center operators toward generating power on site. I've written about the same dynamic before, including with [Atomarine's offshore power ships](/blog/startups/atomarine-floating-data-centers-siting-not-nuclear). Reverion's version is a factory-built unit that ships in a container.

That turns the sales pitch from "clean power" into "power without waiting for the grid," and a buyer with a deadline and a bottleneck cares about time-to-power more than about a few points of efficiency. It also means the product is now supply-constrained. If demand is real, output is the limit, and a tenfold capacity jump is the company's answer to that.

## Credit where it's due

A $175 million round from a syndicate that includes an insurer and a national development bank vehicle is a serious vote from investors who do real diligence on industrial assets. The company also says it has fuel cell plants in customer operation today, which puts it past the stage where most hardware startups are still arguing about a prototype. [Hydrogen Insight](https://www.hydrogeninsight.com/power/german-start-up-that-makes-reversible-solid-oxide-electrolysis-power-plants-raises-175m-in-series-b-funding/2-1-2050991) and the other outlets that covered the round report the same core facts as the company release, and I found no conflict between them on the round itself.

## The numbers that are still claims

Here is where I'd press. The 74.2% electrical efficiency is described as coming from live customer operation, but it appears in the coverage only as a company statement, and the same goes for the $2 billion pipeline. I looked for independent commentary and for comparisons with established US fuel-cell makers, and found none so far. The coverage I saw is announcement rewrites. That isn't a criticism of Reverion, which has done nothing unusual by publishing its own figures at a funding announcement. It's a statement about how much outside verification exists today, which is close to none.

A pipeline is also a different thing from a backlog. The coverage I reviewed doesn't say what share of that $2 billion is contracted, what deposits sit behind it, or whether any named data center operator has signed. Those are the terms that tell you whether a pipeline turns into revenue, and none of them are public yet.

## The diligence question moves to the factory

For a hardware founder, the useful lesson is about what the next round of questions looks like. Once a technology runs in the field and the market pulls on it, investors stop asking whether it works and start asking whether you can build 250 megawatts of it a year. That is a different skill set: site selection, permitting, supplier qualification, yield, and hiring toward that 800-job figure without letting quality slip. [Form Energy's financing of its West Virginia plant](/blog/startups/form-energy-tax-credit-debt-manufacturing-scale-up) is a cleaner illustration of how seriously capital now treats the factory as the asset.

Going from container-scale units to a plant that makes ten times as many is where plenty of hardware companies find out their unit economics were a function of hand assembly. I'm not predicting that for Reverion, and I have no evidence either way. I'm saying it's the test, and it's the one the round is priced on.

## What to watch

Four things would tell us quickly whether the story holds. The factory site and permitting timeline. A named data center customer. Third-party validation of the 74.2% efficiency figure. And how much of the $2 billion pipeline converts to signed contracts. Until a couple of those land, this is a well-funded company with a credible product and a plan, which is a good place to be, but it is not yet proof.
