Fortem Technologies closed a [$50M Series B on September 30](https://dronelife.com/2026/09/30/fortem-technologies-funding-50m-series-b/) led by Lockheed Martin, and the most informative number in the story is not the $50M but the $1.011B: the estimated starting ceiling of a Department of Homeland Security (DHS) counter-drone contract Fortem won as a prime in August. My claim is that the Lockheed cheque matters because of where Lockheed sits relative to that contract, and that the ceiling, being a ceiling, tells us less than it appears to.

<!--more-->

## What Fortem has done

Fortem, based in Utah and founded in 2016, makes SkyDome, a counter-drone system that combines TrueView radar, AI command-and-control software and DroneHunter interceptor drones. Lockheed [committed $25M in April](https://news.lockheedmartin.com/2026-04-22-Lockheed-Martin-Invests-25M-in-Fortem-Technologies-to-Meet-Urgent-Demand-for-Countering-UAS-Threats?asPDF=1) with at least another $25M to be raised, and the second tranche brought in Unusual Machines and unnamed strategic and institutional investors, per [Business Wire](https://www.morningstar.com/news/business-wire/20260930298011/fortem-technologies-closes-50m-series-b-financing-led-by-lockheed-martin). The money goes to more production at the Lindon, Utah plant and wider SkyDome deployment.

Credit where it is earned: being selected as a prime on a five-year DHS contract, reportedly the only small-business prime on it, is hard to do, and it is the difference between a counter-drone company with demos and one with a purchasing channel. Fortem also has a prime contractor willing to put its own money in twice in six months. Few startups get either.

## The ceiling is not revenue

The weak point is arithmetic. A $1.011B ceiling is the most DHS could spend through the vehicle, not what it will, and a prime on a vehicle with other primes presumably still has to win individual task orders. I found trade write-ups of the round and no critique of its size against a ceiling that is not guaranteed spend, so this is my own caution, not a reported concern. The company also describes itself as the only kinetic countermeasure authorized in the US for counter-UAS (unmanned aircraft systems), which is Fortem's own claim and one I could not verify.

So the number to watch is the first DHS task orders under the contract, which will show how much of the ceiling turns into spend. Until then the contract is an option, a valuable one, and the round should be read as financing the capacity to exercise it. The Lindon plant is the sensible place for the money: if orders do arrive, production is what limits Fortem's response.

## Why the investor matters as much as the cash

In defense the sequence seems to be running as prime investment, then contract vehicle, then scale-up capital, and that makes a strategic investor a distribution channel as much as a source of funds. A $50M round is modest next to the sector's megadeals, so for a small defense founder the lesson is to look for a prime's cheque and a contract vehicle together, because the vehicle is what converts a ceiling into revenue.

My own addition, and it is a judgment rather than something in the reporting: a strategic lead can also narrow who might acquire you later, since a rival prime may hesitate to buy a company its competitor helped fund. That is worth negotiating early, in rights and observer seats, not discovering at exit.

## Blackstone took the other road

A day later, on October 1, Blackstone announced [more than $1B of debt and equity](https://qz.com/blackstone-falcata-defense-interceptor-company-100126) to launch Falcata, formed by combining Technology Service Corp and Applied Systems Engineering Inc. to supply sensing, guidance, navigation and control for interceptors. [Blackstone's release](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/articles/blackstone-announces-significant-investment-launch-130000703.html) names Brandon Wolfson as CEO and Jonathan Moneymaker as chairman, with further acquisitions planned. Blackstone's David Kaden said interceptor availability has been a choke point and that navigation and guidance are among the costliest components; the firm says it has put about $20B into the sector over five years.

This is a different bet, and I would not force it into Fortem's frame. Lockheed backed a startup and rode a contract vehicle; Blackstone bought established firms that already hold guidance expertise. The framing in the coverage I found, such as [Alternatives Watch](https://www.alternativeswatch.com/2026/10/02/blackstone-falcata-missile-interceptor-supplier/), is private equity entering defense by acquisition, and I did not find a skeptical piece on whether a roll-up of two established firms can deliver the lower-cost guidance it promises. That is an open question, not a prediction.

For founders making seekers, navigation or sensors, it cuts both ways: a very well-funded buyer now exists, and so does a very well-funded competitor. I would want to know whether Falcata plans to buy components from startups or replace them, and its next acquisition will say a lot.

## What to watch

Three things: Fortem's first DHS task orders, any further Series B closings beyond the $50M, and Falcata's next acquisition or first interceptor program award. If the task orders are large, the Lockheed-plus-vehicle model gets its proof. If they are slow, the round will look like capacity built ahead of demand.
